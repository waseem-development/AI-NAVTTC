
# Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA): Complete Guide

---

## 1. What Is EDA?

Exploratory Data Analysis is the process of studying a dataset before doing anything formal with it — no modelling, no predictions yet. The goal is to simply understand what you have.

Think of it like reading a book before writing a review. You need to know the content, the structure, and the story before you can say anything meaningful about it.

---

## 2. How to Approach a New Dataset

### Step 1: Feel the Data (Read It Carefully, More Than Once)

Before running any code, sit with the data. Read through it slowly the first time, then go back and read it again. This is not wasted time. Every time you revisit a dataset, you notice something you missed before.

* First pass: get a general sense of what is here
* Second pass: start asking questions — what looks odd? what is missing? what do these columns actually mean?

### Step 2: Find Out Who Collected This Data

This matters more than most people think. The person or team who gathered the data had a specific purpose in mind. That purpose shapes everything — what they chose to record, what they ignored, and what errors they might have introduced.

Ask: who made this? For what reason? Was it scraped from the web, collected via a survey, generated by sensors, or pulled from a database? Each source has its own typical problems.

### Step 3: Understand What the Data Is About

What is the subject of this dataset? Is it about customers, patients, stock prices, weather, students? Once you know the topic, you can start applying real-world knowledge to spot things that do not make sense — for example, a person with age 300, or a temperature of -500 degrees.

### Step 4: Study the Metadata

Metadata is data about data. This includes:

* Column names and what they represent
* Data types (numbers, text, dates, categories)
* Units of measurement (is "salary in dollars or thousands of dollars?)
* How and when the data was collected
* Any data dictionary or documentation that comes with the dataset

Never skip reading the documentation. It prevents many mistakes.

### Step 5: Check the Dimensions

Find out how large the dataset is.

* How many rows? (Each row is usually one observation or record)
* How many columns? (Each column is usually one variable or feature)

A dataset with 10 rows and 50 columns has a very different character from one with 10 million rows and 5 columns. Dimension affects every decision you make later.

## 3. The Four Core Properties of Data

### 3.1 Composition

Composition answers: what is this data made of?

* What data types are present? (integers, floats, strings, booleans, dates)
* How many values are missing in each column?
* Are there duplicate rows?
* What are the unique categories in categorical columns?
* Are the values in a column what they claim to be? (For example, a column called "Age" should not contain text)

This is essentially an audit of the raw material.

### 3.2 Correlation

Correlation answers: do two variables move together?

Correlation is measured on a scale from -1 to +1.

**At 0 — No Relationship**

The two variables have no linear connection. Knowing the value of one tells you nothing about the other.

```
Y
|
|    . .  . .  .  .
|  .  .  .  . .  .
| .  .  .   .  .
|
+----------------------------- X
```

**At +1 — Perfect Positive Relationship**

As one variable increases, the other also increases in a straight line.

```
Y
|                    .
|                .
|            .
|        .
|    .
| .
+----------------------------- X
```

**At -1 — Perfect Negative Relationship**

As one variable increases, the other decreases in a straight line.

```
Y
| .
|    .
|        .
|            .
|                .
|                    .
+----------------------------- X
```

**Measuring Correlation — Pearson's R and Spearman's R**

These are two formulas that calculate the correlation value between two columns.

* **Pearson's R** is used when both variables are continuous numbers and the relationship is assumed to be linear. It is the standard default.
* **Spearman's R** is used when data is ranked, ordinal, or when the relationship may not be perfectly linear. It is more robust to outliers.

Both produce a value between -1 and +1.

**Interpreting the value:**

| Range              | Meaning                               |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------- |
| Above +0.5         | Strong positive correlation           |
| Between 0 and +0.5 | Weak to moderate positive correlation |
| 0                  | No linear correlation                 |
| Between -0.5 and 0 | Weak to moderate negative correlation |
| Below -0.5         | Strong negative correlation           |

**Important:** Correlation does not mean causation. Two things can move together without one causing the other.

### 3.3 Comparison

Comparison answers: how do groups or categories differ from each other?

For example:

* Do men and women in this dataset have different average salaries?
* Do customers from different cities buy more or less frequently?
* Did sales go up or down after a campaign?

You are looking for meaningful differences between subgroups or across time periods.

### 3.4 Distribution

Distribution answers: what does the spread of values look like for a single variable?

* Where is the centre? (mean, median)
* How spread out are the values? (range, standard deviation)
* Is the data skewed to the left or right?
* Are there outliers — values that are far from the rest?
* Does the data follow a normal (bell curve) shape, or something else?

Understanding distribution is critical before applying any statistical method, because most methods make assumptions about the shape of the data.

## 4. Types of Data Analytics

EDA sits within a broader landscape of analytics. Here are the four types, in order from simplest to most advanced:

### 4.1 Descriptive Analytics

**Question it answers:** What happened?

This is the most basic level. You summarise and describe what the data shows. It includes counts, averages, percentages, and charts. No predictions, no explanations — just a clear picture of the past.

EDA is primarily descriptive analytics.

Example: "Last month, 3,200 customers made a purchase. The average order value was $47."

### 4.2 Diagnostic Analytics

**Question it answers:** Why did it happen?

Here you dig deeper to find causes. You look for relationships, compare groups, and investigate anomalies to explain why something occurred.

Example: "Sales dropped in March because we had a server outage during the busiest week."

### 4.3 Predictive Analytics

**Question it answers:** What is likely to happen next?

This uses historical patterns and statistical or machine learning models to forecast future outcomes. It moves beyond description into probability.

Example: "Based on past behaviour, this customer has a 78% chance of churning in the next 30 days."

### 4.4 Prescriptive Analytics

**Question it answers:** What should we do about it?

This is the most advanced type. It not only predicts outcomes but recommends specific actions to optimise a result. It often involves optimisation algorithms and decision models.

Example: "To reduce churn, send a discount offer to the 500 high-risk customers identified by the model."

## 5. Key Terms Clarified

These terms are often used interchangeably but they have distinct meanings:

### Data Exploration

The early, open-ended phase of looking at data without a fixed agenda. You are asking broad questions: what is here? what patterns do I see? what looks unusual? It is informal and curiosity-driven.

EDA is structured data exploration — exploration with a systematic method behind it.

### Data Wrangling

The process of reshaping and restructuring data to make it usable. This includes:

* Changing column names
* Splitting one column into two (for example, splitting a "Full Name" column into "First Name" and "Last Name")
* Combining multiple datasets into one
* Converting data types (for example, turning a column of text dates into actual date objects)
* Reformatting values for consistency

Wrangling is about structure and shape. It does not deal with errors or quality — only organisation.

### Data Munging

Munging (also spelled "mungeing") is essentially another word for wrangling, used more in informal or engineering contexts. Some people use "munging" to describe a messier, more ad-hoc version of wrangling — where you are dealing with raw, difficult data from unusual sources.

In practice: if someone says they are "munging" data, they mean they are doing the grubby work of getting raw data into a usable format.

### Data Pre-Processing

Pre-processing is the broader preparation phase that happens before feeding data into a machine learning model. It includes:

* Encoding categorical variables (converting text labels into numbers)
* Scaling or normalising numerical features
* Handling class imbalance
* Splitting data into training and test sets
* Feature selection and engineering

Pre-processing is model-preparation. It assumes the data is already clean and structured; its job is to format it correctly for algorithms.

### Data Cleaning

Cleaning deals specifically with errors and quality problems. This includes:

* Removing or imputing missing values
* Correcting typos and inconsistencies (for example, "Male", "male", "M" all meaning the same thing)
* Removing duplicate records
* Fixing impossible or clearly wrong values (for example, a person born in 1850)
* Handling outliers

Cleaning is about accuracy and reliability. A clean dataset is one you can trust to reflect reality.

---

## 6. How These Fit Together

A typical data project moves through these phases in roughly this order:

1. **Exploration** — understand what you have
2. **Cleaning** — fix errors and quality issues
3. **Wrangling / Munging** — reshape and restructure
4. **Pre-Processing** — prepare for modelling
5. **Modelling** — apply machine learning or statistics
6. **Analytics** — interpret results (descriptive through prescriptive)

In practice, steps 1 through 4 overlap and cycle back. You often discover a cleaning problem while wrangling, or find a structural issue during exploration. The process is iterative, not strictly linear.

---

## Final Note

EDA is not a step you rush through to get to the "real" work. It is the foundation on which every later decision depends. A model trained on poorly understood data will produce poor results no matter how sophisticated the algorithm. The analysts who invest time in genuinely understanding their data before processing it are the ones who build reliable, trustworthy analyses.
