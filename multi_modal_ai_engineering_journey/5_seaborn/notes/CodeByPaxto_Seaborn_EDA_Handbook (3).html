PART 1

Seaborn Mental Model

Seaborn is a statistical plotting layer on Matplotlib. You hand it a tidy DataFrame and column names; it handles grouping (hue), aggregation, confidence intervals, density estimates and default styling, then draws with Matplotlib objects. Analogy: Matplotlib is a workshop of raw tools; Seaborn is a set of jigs for common statistical cuts.

Matplotlib  → low-level, total control, you compute everything
Seaborn     → high-level, "plot this relationship by group", stats built in
Together    → Seaborn draws, Matplotlib finishes (titles, limits, annotations, layout)

Use Seaborn for fast exploration of grouped/statistical views. Use Matplotlib for unusual layouts or full control. Use both almost always in practice:

import numpy as np, pandas as pd, seaborn as sns, matplotlib.pyplot as plt
sns.set_theme(style="whitegrid", context="notebook")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 5))
sns.scatterplot(data=df, x="age", y="income", hue="gender", ax=ax)
ax.set(title="Age vs Income", xlabel="Age", ylabel="Income")
plt.tight_layout()

g.set_axis_labels("Delay (min)", "Flights")
PART 2

Core Functions

Format per family: question answered → key parameters → pitfall → how to read it.

Relational: scatterplot, lineplot

scatterplot — relationship between two numeric variables. Params: hue, style, size, alpha, s. Pitfall: overplotting on large data → lower alpha, sample, or use histplot(x=,y=). Read: direction, strength, shape (non-linear?), clusters, outliers.

sns.scatterplot(data=df, x="Distance", y="ArrDelay", alpha=0.1)

lineplot — change in y over an ordered x (usually time). If several y per x, it aggregates to the mean and draws a bootstrapped 95% interval. Params: estimator, errorbar (("ci",95), "sd", None), units+estimator=None for one line per entity. Pitfall: unsorted or duplicate x silently averaged. Read: trend, seasonality, and the band width as uncertainty.

sns.lineplot(data=df, x="month", y="DepDelay", hue="Reporting_Airline", errorbar=None)

Distributions: histplot, kdeplot, ecdfplot, rugplot

histplot — shape of one variable. bins (or binwidth), stat (count, probability, density, percent), kde=True, hue, multiple (layer, stack, dodge, fill), element (bars, step), common_norm=False (normalise each group separately so unequal group sizes are comparable), log_scale for skewed data.

kdeplot — smoothed density; fill, bw_adjust, common_norm, cut=0. Pitfall: KDE invents density beyond the data range and can hide small samples. ecdfplot — cumulative share ≤ x; no binning, best for comparing groups and reading percentiles. rugplot — tick per observation, add to a KDE to show the real data.

sns.histplot(data=df, x="ArrDelay", hue="Reporting_Airline", element="step",
             stat="density", common_norm=False, binwidth=10)
sns.ecdfplot(data=df, x="ArrDelay", hue="Reporting_Airline")

Read: centre, spread, skew, multiple modes, tails. Right-skewed data → mean > median.

Categorical

countplot vs barplot (key distinction)

countplot → counts rows per category        (only x or y; no numeric variable)
barplot   → aggregates a numeric variable   (mean by default, with error bar)
sns.countplot(data=df, y="Origin", order=df["Origin"].value_counts().index[:10])
sns.barplot(data=df, x="Reporting_Airline", y="ArrDelay", estimator="mean", errorbar=("ci", 95))

A barplot of means hides the distribution. Pair it with a box/violin plot.

  • boxplot: median, IQR, whiskers (1.5×IQR), points beyond as outliers. Compact, many groups.
  • violinplot: boxplot + density shape; shows bimodality. inner="quart", cut=0, split=True with a two-level hue.
  • stripplot: every point (use jitter, alpha); small/medium n.
  • swarmplot: non-overlapping points; only for small n (slow, overflows otherwise).
  • pointplot: estimate + interval per category connected by lines; good for comparing change across a second categorical (e.g. hue=year).

Tip: catplot(kind="box"/"violin"/"bar"/"count"/"strip"/"swarm"/"point", col=...) facets any of these.

Regression: regplot, lmplot

Scatter plus fitted model with a bootstrapped confidence band. regplot is axes-level; lmplot is figure-level (hue, col). Useful params: order=2 (polynomial), lowess=True (non-parametric trend, no CI), robust=True (less sensitive to outliers), logistic=True (binary y), ci, scatter_kws={"alpha":.3}, line_kws.

sns.regplot(data=wb, x="log_gdp_pc", y="life_exp", lowess=True, scatter_kws={"alpha": .5})

A trend line shows association, not causation: confounders, reverse causation and aggregation effects all produce lines. Compare linear vs lowess; a big gap means the relationship is not linear.

Matrix: heatmap, clustermap

corr = df.select_dtypes("number").corr()          # Pearson by default; try method="spearman"
mask = np.triu(np.ones_like(corr, dtype=bool))
sns.heatmap(corr, mask=mask, annot=True, fmt=".2f", cmap="coolwarm", center=0, vmin=-1, vmax=1)
sns.clustermap(corr, cmap="coolwarm", center=0, vmin=-1, vmax=1)

annot prints values, fmt formats them, center=0 with a diverging cmap makes sign visible, vmin/vmax fix the scale, mask hides redundancy, clustermap reorders variables so related ones sit together. Useful for ~5–25 numeric variables. Meaningless with too many variables, with ID/categorical-coded columns, or when relationships are non-linear (Pearson misses them). Also works for pivot_table output (e.g. hour × weekday of mean delay).

Multi-plot: relplot, displot, catplot, pairplot, jointplot

  • relplot / displot / catplot: the figure-level versions of the families above; add row=, col=, col_wrap=, height=, aspect= to split by category (small multiples beat one overloaded plot).
  • pairplot: scatter matrix of all numeric columns; use vars=[...], hue, corner=True, kind="reg", diag_kind="kde". Limit to ≤ 6–8 variables and sample big data.
  • jointplot: bivariate plot with marginal distributions; kind="scatter"|"hist"|"kde"|"reg"|"hex".
sns.relplot(data=wx, x="doy", y="tmax", col="station", hue="year", kind="line", height=3, aspect=1.4)
sns.pairplot(df[["DepDelay","ArrDelay","Distance","AirTime"]].sample(5000), corner=True)

hue = colour groups inside a panel; row/col = separate panels; height/aspect = size of each panel (width = height × aspect). Limitation: they own the figure, so combine with other plots via a manual grid instead.

Styling

sns.set_theme(style="whitegrid", context="talk", palette="deep")   # one call sets all
sns.set_style("ticks"); sns.set_context("paper"); sns.set_palette("colorblind")
sns.color_palette("viridis", 6); sns.despine()
sns.move_legend(ax, "upper left", bbox_to_anchor=(1, 1))

Styles: darkgrid, whitegrid, dark, white, ticks. Contexts scale fonts: paper < notebook < talk < poster. Palettes: qualitative for categories, sequential for ordered/magnitude, diverging for centred data (correlation). Customise for readability (colour-blind-safe, larger fonts for slides); stop when changes add decoration but no information.

Visual Chart Guide (5 examples per chart)

Simple wording: what each chart is, when to use or avoid it, 5 code examples on different data, parameters and gotchas. Diagrams use made-up demo data to show each chart's shape.

import numpy as np, pandas as pd, seaborn as sns, matplotlib.pyplot as plt
sns.set_theme(style="whitegrid")
# df = flights (BTS), wx = weather (NOAA), wb = country-year data (World Bank), fred = time series (FRED)

1. Histogram — histplot

Idea: Sort values into buckets (bins) and count each bucket. Like sorting coins into jars.

Use when: you want the shape of ONE number: centre, spread, skew, outliers.

Avoid when: comparing many groups in one plot (use box/ECDF), or tiny data (few bars lie).

sns.histplot(data=df, x="ArrDelay", binwidth=10)                                   # 1 flights: delay shape
sns.histplot(data=df, x="ArrDelay", hue="Reporting_Airline", element="step",
             stat="density", common_norm=False)                                     # 2 airlines, fair compare
sns.histplot(data=wb, x="gdp_pc", log_scale=True)                                   # 3 skewed money data
sns.histplot(data=wx, x="TMAX", hue="station", multiple="stack")                    # 4 weather by station
sns.histplot(x=ret, stat="probability", bins=60, kde=True)                          # 5 stock returns

Parameters: bins/binwidth = bucket size · stat = count, density, probability, percent · hue = groups · multiple = layer, stack, dodge, fill · element = bars or step · common_norm=False = scale each group separately · log_scale · kde=True = smooth line.

2. Scatter — scatterplot

Idea: One dot per row, x and y are two numbers.

Use when: asking "do these two numbers move together?"; also to spot clusters and outliers.

Avoid when: one axis is a category (use box/bar), or you have 500k+ points without alpha/sampling.

sns.scatterplot(data=df, x="Distance", y="AirTime")                                 # 1 flights: distance vs time
sns.scatterplot(data=df.sample(5000), x="DepDelay", y="ArrDelay", alpha=0.2)        # 2 big data: use alpha
sns.scatterplot(data=wb, x="gdp_pc", y="life_exp", hue="region"); plt.xscale("log") # 3 countries
sns.scatterplot(data=wb, x="gdp_pc", y="life_exp", size="population",
                sizes=(20, 400), alpha=.6)                                          # 4 3rd variable = size
sns.scatterplot(data=wx, x="dewpoint", y="TMAX", hue="season", style="station")     # 5 weather

Parameters: hue (colour group or number) · style (marker shape) · size + sizes=(min,max) · alpha · s (fixed size) · marker.

Gotchas: Overplotting hides data. A pattern is NOT proof one thing causes the other. Don't encode 4 variables at once (colour + style + size gets unreadable).

3. Line — lineplot

Idea: Connect points in order. If many y values share one x, Seaborn plots the mean and a shaded 95% interval.

Use when: x is ordered, mostly time: trends, seasons, before/after.

Avoid when: x has no natural order (use bars) or you have just 2 points.

sns.lineplot(data=wx, x="doy", y="TMAX")                                            # 1 yearly temperature cycle
sns.lineplot(data=df, x="month", y="DepDelay", hue="Reporting_Airline")             # 2 airlines by month
sns.lineplot(data=fred, x="date", y="unrate")                                       # 3 unemployment over time
sns.lineplot(data=wb, x="year", y="life_exp", units="country",
             estimator=None, alpha=.3)                                              # 4 one line per country
sns.lineplot(x=vol.index, y=vol.values)                                             # 5 rolling volatility

Parameters: hue, style · estimator (mean default; None = raw) · errorbar (("ci",95), "sd", None) · units · markers=True.

4. Box — boxplot

Idea: A 5-number summary of a number for each category: min whisker, Q1, median, Q3, max whisker (outliers beyond 1.5×IQR shown as dots).

Use when: comparing a number across categories, especially skewed data or when outliers matter.

Avoid when: you need the exact shape (two bumps?), use violinplot; or sample is tiny, use stripplot.

sns.boxplot(data=df, x="Reporting_Airline", y="ArrDelay", showfliers=False)         # 1 delay by airline
sns.boxplot(data=wx, x="month", y="TMAX")                                           # 2 monthly temperature
sns.boxplot(data=df, y="Origin", x="DepDelay", order=top10)                         # 3 horizontal, top airports
sns.boxplot(data=wb, x="region", y="life_exp")                                      # 4 regions
sns.boxplot(data=wx, x="station", y="PRCP", hue="season")                           # 5 two grouping levels

Parameters: order · hue · showfliers · whis (whisker length) · orient · width. Add sns.stripplot(..., color="k", alpha=.2) on top to show the real points.

5. Count vs Bar — countplot vs barplot

Rule: counting rows → countplot. Summarising a number column → barplot.

Use when: comparing a few categories; bars start at 0 so lengths are honest.

Avoid when: 30+ categories (use top-N, horizontal), or you need spread (bars of averages hide it, add a boxplot).

# countplot
sns.countplot(data=df, x="Reporting_Airline")                                       # 1 flights per airline
sns.countplot(data=df, y="Origin", order=df["Origin"].value_counts().index[:10])    # 2 top 10 airports
sns.countplot(data=df, x="Cancelled")                                               # 3 cancelled vs not
sns.countplot(data=wx, x="month", hue="station")                                    # 4 observations per month
sns.countplot(data=wb, x="income_group")                                            # 5 countries per group
# barplot
sns.barplot(data=df, x="Reporting_Airline", y="ArrDelay")                           # 1 mean delay (+95% CI)
sns.barplot(data=df, x="Reporting_Airline", y="ArrDelay",
            estimator="median", errorbar=None)                                      # 2 median, no bars of error
sns.barplot(data=wx, x="month", y="PRCP", estimator="sum", errorbar=None)           # 3 total rain per month
sns.barplot(data=wb, x="region", y="gdp_pc", errorbar="sd")                         # 4 spread as std dev
sns.barplot(data=df, x="weekday", y="ArrDelay", hue="Reporting_Airline")            # 5 two groups

Parameters: order, hue, orient · barplot only: estimator ("mean", "median", "sum", a function), errorbar ("ci", "sd", "se", None).

6. Heatmap — heatmap

Idea: A grid where colour shows a number. Like a weather map but for a table.

Use when: looking at a correlation matrix, a pivot table (hour × weekday), or where data is missing.

Avoid when: 40+ variables (unreadable), or columns are IDs/codes; Pearson misses curved relationships (check scatter).

sns.heatmap(df.corr(numeric_only=True), annot=True, fmt=".2f", cmap="coolwarm",
            center=0, vmin=-1, vmax=1)                                              # 1 correlations
corr = wb.corr(numeric_only=True)
sns.heatmap(corr, mask=np.triu(np.ones_like(corr, dtype=bool)), annot=True)         # 2 hide duplicate half
sns.heatmap(df.pivot_table(index="hour", columns="weekday", values="ArrDelay"),
            cmap="rocket_r")                                                        # 3 delay by hour and day
sns.heatmap(wx.pivot_table(index="year", columns="month", values="TMAX"),
            cmap="coolwarm")                                                        # 4 climate calendar
sns.heatmap(df.isna(), cbar=False)                                                  # 5 where is data missing?

Parameters: annot (show numbers) · fmt (number format) · cmap · center · vmin/vmax · mask · linewidths. Use sns.clustermap(corr) to group related variables.

PART 4

Seaborn + Matplotlib

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(15, 4), sharey=False)
sns.histplot(data=df, x="ArrDelay", ax=axes[0])
sns.boxplot(data=df, x="Reporting_Airline", y="ArrDelay", showfliers=False, ax=axes[1])
sns.scatterplot(data=df, x="Distance", y="ArrDelay", alpha=.1, ax=axes[2])
axes[0].set(title="Delay distribution", xlabel="Arrival delay (min)")
axes[1].tick_params(axis="x", rotation=45)
axes[2].set(xlim=(0, 3000), ylim=(-60, 240))
axes[2].axhline(0, color="gray", ls="--")
axes[2].annotate("on time", xy=(2500, 0), xytext=(2000, 80), arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="->"))
fig.suptitle("Flight delays", y=1.02)
plt.tight_layout()

fig = canvas (size, suptitle, saving: fig.savefig("x.png", dpi=200, bbox_inches="tight")); ax = one plot; ax= tells Seaborn where to draw; ax.set(...) sets title/labels/limits in one call; tight_layout() fixes overlaps. For figure-level plots use g.ax / g.axes.flat / g.figure.

Choosing the Right Visualization

QuestionPlotAlternatives / notes
Distribution of one numeric variable?histplot (+kde)ecdfplot for percentiles; boxplot for summary; log_scale if skewed
Compare distributions across groups?boxplot / violinplotoverlaid ecdfplot; histplot with common_norm=False
Observations per category?countplotvalue_counts().plot; sort with order
Average/total of a number per category?barplotpointplot for several groups; check distribution too
Two numeric variables?scatterplothexbin/2D histplot when dense; regplot for trend
Change over time?lineplotrelplot facets; rolling means for noisy data
Seasonal pattern?lineplot by month/year, or heatmap of pivotboxplot per month
Which numeric variables are related?heatmap of corr()clustermap for many; pairplot to check shape
Many numeric variables at once?pairplot (≤ 8 vars)heatmap first, then zoom in

Same plot per segment?row/col facetsbetter than 12 hues
Outliers?boxplot, stripplot, ecdfplotthen inspect the rows in Pandas
Variable types?
 one numeric          → histplot / ecdfplot / boxplot
 one categorical      → countplot
 numeric × numeric    → scatterplot (time on x? → lineplot) → trend? regplot

Multiple plots are often right: barplot for the headline mean, boxplot for spread, ecdfplot for tails. Pick the one that answers the question with least ambiguity.

PART 6

Practical EDA Workflow

Question → understand dataset (source, units, grain) → inspect (shape, dtypes, head, describe, isna)
→ clean (types, dates, duplicates, missing, impossible values)
→ univariate (hist/box/count) → bivariate (scatter/box/bar/line) → multivariate (hue, facets, heatmap)
→ outliers (explain: error, rare event, or signal?) → patterns → insights → communicate

Rule: each chart answers a written question. If you cannot state the question, do not make the chart. Write one sentence of insight per figure.

Patterns worth reusing

  • Univariate scan: loop numeric columns into a subplots grid of histplot.
  • Rank then plot: compute groupby, sort, pass as order.
  • Seasonality: extract month/dayofyear, plot with hue=year.
  • Missingness: sns.heatmap(df.isna(), cbar=False) to see patterns.
  • Pivot heatmap: sns.heatmap(df.pivot_table(index="hour", columns="weekday", values="delay")).

Example A — Which airlines have the highest delays? (flights)

Variables: airline (categorical), arrival delay (numeric, skewed). Plot: boxplot ordered by median, because delay is skewed and a barplot of means hides the tails. Interpret: compare medians and IQRs, then check tails with an ECDF.

order = df.groupby("Reporting_Airline")["ArrDelay"].median().sort_values().index

sns.ecdfplot(data=df, x="ArrDelay", hue="Reporting_Airline")   # tails: share delayed > 60 min

Variables: date → month/day-of-year, daily max temperature. Plot: boxplot per month (spread and seasonality) plus lineplot by day-of-year with year as hue. Interpret: peak month, within-month variability, whether recent years sit above older years.

Variables: daily index level → returns → rolling standard deviation. Plot: lineplot of rolling volatility (time question); histplot of returns for shape. Interpret: volatility clusters, spikes in stress periods; return distributions have fatter tails than a normal.

Three EDA Projects (no solutions)

Each report must contain: 1 Problem/Objective · 2 Dataset & Source · 3 Data Understanding · 4 Data Cleaning · 5 Exploratory Analysis · 6 Statistical Findings · 7 Visualizations · 8 Key Insights · 9 Limitations · 10 Final Conclusions. A good submission states a question for every figure, justifies each chart choice, reports numbers (not just pictures), separates observation from interpretation, and lists limitations honestly.

Project 1 — Beginner: US Flight On-Time Performance

Understand data → clean → basic statistics → Pandas → basic visualisation → simple insights

Source: BTS Reporting Carrier On-Time Performance (one or a few months; sample if large) or a derived subset such as nycflights13. Background: scheduled vs actual times, delays in minutes, cancellations. Objective: your first complete real-world EDA.

Questions: Which airlines have the highest average delay? What does the delay distribution look like? Are delays related to departure hour? Which airports have the most delays? Does distance relate to delay? Which months or days are worst? Are there extreme outliers, and are they errors or real events? How do cancellations compare with delays?

Checklist: load and inspect, fix dtypes and dates, handle cancelled flights and missing delays (decide and document), derive hour/month/weekday columns, compute mean, median, variance, std, percentiles, IQR, flag outliers with NumPy, use groupby/agg/value_counts/describe, produce histplot, boxplot, barplot, countplot, scatterplot, lineplot and a heatmap (hour × weekday).

Hints: mean vs median will disagree, ask why. Use np.percentile or quantile for the IQR rule. Delay can be negative (early); do not drop those blindly.

Deliverables: cleaned dataset, notebook, figures, statistical summary, written findings and conclusions. Stretch: (1) define "on time" as ≤ 15 min and compare rates; (2) rank airports by delay with a minimum flight count; (3) check whether distance explains delay once you control for hour; (4) one-page executive summary; (5) list biases (e.g. only one period).

Project 2 — Intermediate: NOAA Weather & Climate

Messier data → cleaning → time analysis → grouping → distributions → relationships → outliers → deeper insights

Source: NOAA NCEI GHCN-Daily for 2–3 stations in contrasting climates and 10+ years (add hourly ISD data if you want humidity via dew point and pressure). Objective: time-based and comparative analysis on imperfect data.

Questions: How does temperature change through the year? Which months get the most precipitation? How variable is temperature, and does variability differ by season or station? Is humidity (or dew point) related to temperature? Which observations are unusual (heat waves, extreme rainfall)? How do stations differ? Is there a visible multi-year trend?

Checklist: read metadata and units, parse dates, reshape if long-format, quantify and handle missing days (and flagged/sentinel values), engineer month/season/year, compare stations, build a daily climatology and anomaly (value − climatological mean), rolling means, correlations, outlier analysis per season. Visualise with lineplot, relplot facets, boxplot by month, histplot/ecdfplot, scatterplot, heatmap (year × month). For every chart, write why you chose it.

Hints: compare precipitation totals and number of wet days, they tell different stories. Beware spurious trends from changing missing-data patterns.

Stretch: (1) ranking of extreme events by percentile; (2) compare first vs last decade distributions; (3) lagged correlation (does today's temperature relate to tomorrow's?); (4) a faceted station × season figure; (5) discuss station-move and measurement biases.

Project 3 — Advanced: Economic / Financial Analysis

Public data → acquisition → multiple indicators → cleaning → transformation → statistics → advanced visuals → interpretation → analytical story

Source: World Bank WDI (and optionally FRED). Suggested coherent problem: How are income, inflation, unemployment and life expectancy related across countries, and how has that changed over time? (Or a markets variant: FRED index levels, rates and CPI over time.) Pick one problem, 4–6 indicators, ~30–60 countries, 20+ years.

Questions: How are GDP per capita and life expectancy related, and has the relationship shifted? Do high-inflation countries differ in growth or unemployment? Which indicators move together over time? How do regions/income groups differ? Which countries are outliers, and why? What does correlation here not tell you?

Checklist: acquire (API or CSV), read indicator metadata and units, tidy wide→long with melt, merge indicators on country-year, concat extra sources, handle missing/duplicates/aggregate rows (World Bank files include regional aggregates, remove them), fix dtypes, pivot_table, query, datetime ops, descriptive stats, variance/std/percentiles, correlation and covariance, outlier analysis. Visualise with lineplot, scatterplot, regplot/lmplot, boxplot, histplot, barplot, heatmap and faceted plots.

Hints: use log scale for GDP/population; compare Pearson and Spearman; check one-year cross-section vs pooled panel (pooling can mislead). Write explicitly: correlation ≠ causation, and name at least two confounders for each relationship.

Stretch: (1) rolling correlation over time; (2) decade-by-decade lmplot; (3) clustermap of indicators; (4) add a second dataset (e.g. health expenditure); (5) write an analytical story with limitations and data-quality caveats.
